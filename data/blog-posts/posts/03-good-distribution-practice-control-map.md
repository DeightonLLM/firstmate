A distribution process is easier to discuss when the team can see its handoffs. Map what happens from receipt through delivery, identify who owns each step, and note where the team records an exception. This is a practical operations exercise—not a legal checklist.

The available research includes distributor descriptions of logistics and handling capabilities from McKesson and Morris & Dickson. Those pages explain the companies' own services; they are not official Good Distribution Practice texts and do not establish universal controls. The research session could not reach the attempted European Commission GDP page or WHO publication record. ([McKesson](https://www.mckesson.com/business-solutions/pharmaceutical-medical-products-distribution/); [Morris & Dickson](https://www.morrisdickson.com/))

## Start with product, market and role

Before mapping a process, define which products, locations and organizations are in scope. Expectations can depend on jurisdiction, product and the role a party performs. Do not use this article to decide which laws or standards apply.

## Map the workflow

### Receiving and supplier checks

Describe how your operation identifies an incoming shipment, connects it to the relevant order and supporting information, and routes a mismatch for review. Identify who is responsible for the next action. The details belong in your approved procedures and should be checked against your actual obligations.

### Storage and handling

Record how the operation assigns product locations, controls access, monitors any product-specific conditions and responds to a recorded exception. Use the product's applicable instructions and your established procedures; this article supplies no temperature range or universal storage specification.

### Dispatch and delivery

Map preparation, dispatch, transfer to the next party and evidence of receipt. Note the information that travels with the shipment, the contact for a delay or discrepancy, and the point at which responsibility changes under your agreed process.

## Make procedures usable

A process map should make ownership legible. For each activity, name the responsible role, the record used, the trigger for escalation and the person who can close the issue. Check that staff can find the current approved procedure and know where to record exceptions.

## Learn from deviations

When an exception occurs, document it using the organization's approved process, identify who assesses it and record the outcome and follow-up. This is a general quality-management practice description, not a claim that any particular form or action is legally required in every setting.

## Confirm requirements with your own compliance function

**Important:** This article describes general industry process-mapping practice. It is not regulatory advice and is not a statement of legal requirements. Confirm applicable GDP rules, current official sources, records, retention and review expectations with your own compliance function before adopting or publishing a control map.
