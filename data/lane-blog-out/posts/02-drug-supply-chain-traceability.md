Traceability is often reduced to a barcode or a software demonstration. In practice, a useful traceability conversation has to include the product identity, the parties that handle it, the events recorded as it moves, and what happens when an expected record is missing or inconsistent.

The GS1 US pharmaceutical chain-of-custody guideline describes standardized exchange of serialized product event information, including EPCIS, across supply-chain participants such as manufacturers, repackagers, wholesalers, logistics providers and reverse-logistics partners. HDA also identifies information management as part of healthcare distribution. These sources support the operational framing here; they do not establish the legal requirements for a particular pharmacy or transaction. ([GS1 US guideline](https://www.gs1us.org/industries-and-insights/by-industry/healthcare/standards-in-use/pharmaceutical/chain-of-custody); [HDA](https://www.hda.org/))

## Traceability is a shared process, not just a scanner

A scan can capture or retrieve information, but it does not by itself settle whether two partners interpret the product and event consistently. Teams need to know which identity is being discussed, which partner or location is involved, what event the record represents, and who follows up when data does not line up.

### Identify product and trading partners

Start by agreeing how the product and the organizations or locations involved are identified in the systems used by the parties. The GS1 guideline is a useful standards-oriented reference for chain-of-custody concepts. A standards model should not be mistaken for a complete statement of a jurisdiction's legal duties.

### Record and exchange the event

An event record is useful when the parties understand what happened and can connect it with the relevant product and partner context. EPCIS is discussed in the GS1 US guideline as part of standardized event exchange. For a buyer, the practical questions are whether partner systems can exchange the information they need, how exceptions are surfaced and who owns the resolution.

## Where exceptions surface

A record might be absent, duplicated, delayed or inconsistent with another system. Those are examples of operational exceptions, not a statement about the legal consequences of any particular discrepancy. Agree how staff route the question, preserve relevant records and obtain a response from the responsible partner.

## Readiness questions for each partner

- Which product and partner identifiers are used in the exchange?
- What events are represented, and which party supplies each record?
- How are missing or conflicting records identified?
- Who owns investigation and communication on each side?
- What evidence can the buyer retrieve for its own review?

## U.S. legal requirements need primary-source confirmation

This article does not state DSCSA duties, effective dates, exemptions or transaction-level requirements. The research supporting this draft could not reach the FDA's DSCSA page. Before using this material as compliance guidance, confirm the current law and FDA materials for the relevant product, trading partner and date with your compliance function.
